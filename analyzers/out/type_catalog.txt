# Document classification

You are classifying a single scanned document arriving at a specialty pharmacy hub. It is one of three kinds of work: a new prescription or referral coming in, clinical evidence supporting a prior-authorization request, or correspondence back from a payer. Exactly one document type applies to the whole file. Read the whole document before deciding — the decisive evidence is often a single sentence in the body, not the letterhead or the title.

## The document types

### referralForm
A specialty pharmacy referral, enrolment or start form — the packet a prescriber's office faxes to open a case. Multi-section and field-and-value throughout: patient demographics, medical AND pharmacy insurance, a clinical block with diagnosis and ICD-10 code, a prescription block, shipping or delivery preferences, and prescriber attestation and patient consent signatures. Often drug-specific and branded by the manufacturer or the pharmacy. This is the intake workhorse and it is usually the longest document in the queue.

Look for:
- "Referral Form", "Enrollment Form", "Prescription & Enrollment Form", "Start Form"
- separate Patient Information, Insurance Information and Prescriber Information sections
- both medical plan and pharmacy benefit identifiers on one page (RxBIN, RxPCN, RxGRP)
- a shipping or ship-to section, and delivery preference checkboxes
- patient consent, HIPAA authorisation or assignment-of-benefits block
- "Fax completed form to", multi-page with page 1 of 3 style footers

### prescriptionOrder
A prescription or medication order on its own — written, printed or e-prescribed by a prescriber for one patient. Names a drug with strength and dose form, a quantity, refills, and SIG dosing directions, and carries a prescriber signature line with a DEA or NPI number. Its whole purpose is to order the drug; it does not collect insurance, shipping or consent information.

Look for:
- Rx symbol, "Prescription", "Medication Order", "Rx Order"
- drug name with strength, e.g. "Adalimumab 40 mg/0.8 mL"
- "Sig:", "Directions:", "Dispense:", "Refills:", "Days Supply"
- "Dispense as Written", "Substitution Permitted", DAW checkbox pair
- prescriber signature line with DEA and NPI beneath it
- e-prescription footer such as "Electronically signed" or a Surescripts stamp

### insuranceCard
A scan or photograph of a medical or pharmacy benefit card, usually front and back reproduced on one page. Very short — almost entirely identifier fields with no prose at all. The pharmacy routing numbers are the defining content.

Look for:
- RxBIN, RxPCN, RxGRP printed together
- Member ID, Group Number, plan and payer logo
- copay grid such as "Office Visit $30 / Specialist $60 / ER $250"
- member services and pharmacy help desk phone numbers on the reverse
- a barcode, magnetic stripe image, or "This card does not guarantee coverage"

### clinicalNote
A clinical, progress, office-visit, consultation or discharge note written by a clinician documenting an encounter. Narrative prose organised into clinical sections, describing history, examination, results and a plan, and often carrying an embedded medication list and recent lab values. Its purpose is to record care — it requests nothing and decides nothing.

Look for:
- "Chief Complaint", "HPI", "Review of Systems", "Assessment and Plan"
- "Subjective", "Objective", "Assessment", "Plan" section headers
- past medical history, allergies, current medication list
- prior therapy history — drugs tried, duration, and why they were stopped
- dated encounter with a rendering provider and an EHR footer

### labReport
A laboratory results report issued by a testing laboratory. Dominated by a results table: test name, result value, units, reference range and an abnormal flag, one row per analyte. Carries specimen collection and report dates, an accession number, and the performing laboratory's name and CLIA number. Reports results, interprets nothing.

Look for:
- a results table with a "Reference Range" or "Reference Interval" column
- H, L, A or CRITICAL abnormal flags beside values
- "Accession", "Specimen Collected", "Specimen Received", "Report Status: Final"
- performing laboratory name and CLIA number, laboratory director
- LOINC codes, or "Performed at" with a laboratory address

### letterOfMedicalNecessity
A letter written BY a prescriber TO a payer arguing that a therapy is medically necessary for a named patient. Argumentative in tone: it states the diagnosis, recounts therapies already tried and failed, cites clinical guidance, and asks for coverage. Written before or during an appeal. It is a request, never a decision.

Look for:
- "Letter of Medical Necessity", "LMN", "To Whom It May Concern"
- "I am writing on behalf of my patient", "I am requesting coverage"
- failed prior therapies with dates, durations and reasons for failure
- contraindications to formulary alternatives, step-therapy history
- cited guidelines or labelling, then a closing request for approval
- prescriber letterhead and signature, addressed to a plan or PBM

### paDeterminationLetter
A letter from a payer, plan or PBM communicating the DECISION on a prior-authorization request for one named patient — approved, denied, or approved in part. Letter prose addressed to the patient or the prescriber. An approval carries an authorization number and a validity period; a denial carries a reason and, because it is an adverse determination, is legally required to describe appeal rights and deadlines. Choose this type for BOTH outcomes — the decision itself is captured in the PAOutcome field, not by picking a different type.

Look for:
- "approved", "certified", "authorized", "coverage has been approved"
- "denied", "not approved", "adverse determination", "does not meet criteria"
- authorization number with effective and valid-through dates
- a stated denial reason and the medical policy or criteria it cites
- "you have the right to appeal", an appeal deadline in days
- formulary alternatives suggested, peer-to-peer review offered
- external or independent review organisation contact details

### paResponseFax
A payer prior-authorization response that is a FORM rather than a letter — a fax-back response sheet, a portal determination printout, or an outcome or status notice laid out as fields and boxes. May report approved, denied, partially approved or, very commonly, pending with a list of information the payer still needs. Choose this when the document is clearly a determination or status update but is a form or status report rather than correspondence.

Look for:
- "PA Response", "Determination Notice", "Request Status", "Outcome"
- a status field with a value beside it rather than a decision sentence
- "Additional information required", a list of items to return
- "Fax this page back to", a response-due date
- reviewer name and credentials, callback or nurse-line number
- form, grid or checkbox layout with little or no prose

### explanationOfBenefits
An explanation of benefits or pharmacy claim remittance — a statement about a claim that has ALREADY been adjudicated, showing what was billed, what the plan allowed, what it paid, and what the patient owes. Dominated by dollar amounts and adjustment codes. About money for a claim already processed, not permission for a therapy not yet dispensed.

Look for:
- "Explanation of Benefits", "This is not a bill", "Claim Detail"
- columns for Billed / Allowed / Plan Paid / Patient Responsibility
- copay, coinsurance and deductible amounts in dollars
- claim number and a date of service, with a claim status
- CARC and RARC adjustment codes such as CO-50, CO-197, PR-1, N130
- year-to-date deductible or out-of-pocket accumulator totals

### other
Anything that is none of the nine above — fax cover sheets, blank or illegible pages, financial assistance and copay-card paperwork, shipping manifests, correspondence unrelated to a prior authorization, or a document type not in this list. Choose this rather than forcing a poor fit. Choosing it is correct behaviour, not a failure.

Look for:
- fax banner or cover page with a page count and no clinical content
- unreadable, blank, upside-down, or non-English content
- copay card, foundation grant or patient assistance enrolment
- a genuine document type absent from this list

## Telling confusable types apart

**referralForm vs prescriptionOrder**
This is the most common confusion in the intake queue, because a referral packet CONTAINS a genuine prescription block — drug, strength, SIG, quantity, signature. Decide on the sections around it. If the document also collects insurance identifiers, shipping or delivery preferences, patient consent, or clinical background, it is a referralForm no matter how complete the Rx block is. Choose prescriptionOrder only when the page is the medication order and essentially nothing else.

**insuranceCard vs referralForm**
Both carry Member ID, Group, RxBIN, RxPCN and RxGRP. An insurance card is a reproduction of a physical card — a small bordered rectangle, or two of them, with a logo and no sentences. A referral form is a full-page form in which those identifiers occupy one section among many. If there is a prescriber block, a diagnosis, or a signature line, it is not a card.

**clinicalNote vs letterOfMedicalNecessity**
Both are written by a clinician and both contain clinical narrative. The difference is the audience and the ask. A letter of medical necessity is addressed to a payer and requests coverage. A clinical note documents an encounter and asks for nothing. If it contains "I am writing to request" or is addressed to a plan or PBM, it is a letterOfMedicalNecessity — even though it recounts the same history a note would.

**labReport vs clinicalNote**
Clinical notes routinely quote recent lab values inside the objective section, so the presence of lab data decides nothing. A labReport is ISSUED BY a laboratory: it names the performing lab, an accession number and a specimen collection date, and its body is a results table. If the lab values sit inside narrative prose written by a clinician alongside history and a plan, it is a clinicalNote.

**paDeterminationLetter vs paResponseFax**
Both report a payer determination, so the OUTCOME never decides this — an approval and a denial can each be either type. Decide on format. A letter has a salutation, paragraphs and a closing signature block. A response fax is a form: labelled boxes, a status field, checkboxes, and usually an instruction to fax the sheet back. A pending status with a list of information still required is almost always paResponseFax.

**paDeterminationLetter vs explanationOfBenefits**
Both arrive on payer letterhead and both state an outcome, but they concern different things. A determination is permission for a therapy BEFORE it is dispensed: it names an authorization, a validity period, or appeal rights. An EOB accounts for a claim ALREADY adjudicated: it has dollar columns, a patient-responsibility amount, and CARC or RARC adjustment codes. Dollar amounts for billed and allowed charges are near-conclusive evidence of explanationOfBenefits.

**explanationOfBenefits vs paResponseFax**
A denied claim on an EOB and a denied PA on a response fax both read as a refusal with a code beside it. An EOB settles money — it will show what was billed and what the patient owes. A response fax settles authorization — it will reference the request, the requested drug, and what to submit or where to fax back. If there is no dollar figure anywhere, it is not an EOB.

## How to answer

Read the whole document, then return exactly one type from this list:

`referralForm`, `prescriptionOrder`, `insuranceCard`, `clinicalNote`, `labReport`, `letterOfMedicalNecessity`, `paDeterminationLetter`, `paResponseFax`, `explanationOfBenefits`, `other`

**`evidence`** — one short sentence naming what decided it. If two types are genuinely close, say which you rejected and why, and lower your confidence accordingly. Choosing `other` when nothing fits is correct behaviour, not a failure.

**`evidence_phrases`** — one to three short passages **copied verbatim from the document above**, character for character. These are the proof behind your answer, and they are checked automatically against the document text: a phrase that cannot be found there is recorded as unproven. So copy them — do not retype them from memory, do not tidy the spelling or punctuation, and never stitch together text from two different places, because a stitched phrase is not a real substring and will fail the check even though you read it correctly. A handful of words each is enough. The wording listed under "Look for" above is what to hunt for.

Return the passage text and nothing else. **Do not wrap it in quotation marks**, do not add ellipses or square brackets, and do not prefix it with a dash or a bullet. The check is a plain substring search against the document, so

-  `We've approved your request for coverage.`  is found
-  `"We've approved your request for coverage."`  is NOT found

because the document does not contain the quotation marks you added.

Where the document also contains language belonging to a type you rejected — a denial letter with a prescription attached to it — quote the passages that establish **your** type, not the ones you rejected. Say the rejected type in `evidence` instead.
